History & Vexillology

Early Settlers and Ancient Origins

The ancestors of today's Basque population have lived in the western Pyrenees region for thousands of years. Archaeological evidence demonstrates continuous human occupation from prehistoric periods through the Roman era.

When the Romans expanded into the Iberian Peninsula, local communities maintained aspects of their distinct culture and language. Although Roman political influence reached the region, Euskara survived while many other pre-Roman languages disappeared.

The Basque territories later became connected to medieval kingdoms such as Navarre, helping shape the region's political history.

Loss of Rights and Modern Identity

Historically, many Basque territories enjoyed local legal privileges known as fueros, which allowed significant regional self-government.

During the nineteenth century, political changes in Spain gradually reduced these traditional rights. The twentieth century brought further challenges, particularly during Francisco Franco's dictatorship (1939-1975), when public use of Basque language and symbols was restricted.

Following Spain's transition to democracy, the Basque Autonomous Community gained substantial self-government. Euskara and many cultural traditions experienced a major revival, becoming important elements of contemporary Basque identity.

The Basque Flag (Ikurriña)

The best-known Basque symbol is the Ikurriña, the red, green, and white flag associated with the Basque Country.

The flag was created in 1894 by brothers Sabino and Luis Arana. Initially designed to represent Biscay (Bizkaia), it later became widely adopted as a symbol of the broader Basque people.

The colors are commonly interpreted as:

  • Red: the Basque people.
  • Green: traditions and the historic oak tree of Gernika.
  • White: peace and faith.

Today, the Ikurriña is an official symbol of the Basque Autonomous Community and is displayed throughout the region.
